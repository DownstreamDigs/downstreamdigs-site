The Fossils of the Peace River

Millions of years of Florida's coastline are packed into this riverbed. Here's what's actually down there, and how it got there.

Fifty million years in a riverbed

Florida spent most of its history underwater. As sea levels rose and fell over millions of years, layer after layer of marine sediment built up across the state — what geologists call the Hawthorn Group, and within it, the phosphate-rich Bone Valley Formation that the Peace River cuts straight through.

The Peace is a blackwater river — slow-moving, tannin-stained, full of decaying leaf matter. That slow current is part of what makes it such a good fossil site: it wears down the banks and riverbed gradually instead of scouring everything away, releasing fossils a little at a time. As those layers erode out of the banks and bed, fossils wash downstream and collect in gravel bars, bends, and slow water — exactly where we look.

From warm seas to the last Ice Age

The fossils in the Peace River span an enormous stretch of time — roughly the Miocene Epoch, starting about 23 million years ago, through the end of the Pleistocene, around 11,700 years ago. During the Miocene, Florida sat under a warm, shallow sea thick with sharks, rays, and marine mammals, which is why shark teeth are by far the most common find. By the Pleistocene, sea levels had dropped and Florida looked more like an African savanna than a swamp — home to mammoths, giant sloths, and saber-toothed cats. Fossils from both eras end up mixed together in the same gravel bars today.

What you'll actually find

Shark teeth

By far the most common fossil in the river, and the reason most people start hunting here in the first place. Megalodon is the one everyone's after, but you're far more likely to come home with a handful of smaller teeth — snaggletooth, sand tiger, mako, bull, lemon, and tiger shark, among others. Some of those species are still swimming today; others have been extinct for millions of years.

Ice Age megafauna

Less common, more exciting when it happens. Mastodon and mammoth teeth turn up regularly enough that most serious hunters have found at least one. Bone and teeth from giant ground sloths, dire wolves, glyptodonts (imagine an armadillo the size of a small car), ancient horses, bison, and tapir are all out there too — remnants of the Ice Age animals that once grazed what's now the riverbed.

Marine life

Alongside the sharks, the river holds fossils from dugongs (an ancient relative of the manatee), whales, dolphins, sea turtles, and stingray plates, plus shells and other invertebrates from when this whole stretch of Florida was seafloor.

The one permit you might need. Shark teeth don't require a permit — collect as many as you find. Anything else vertebrate (bone, mastodon and mammoth teeth, and similar) legally requires a Florida Fossil Permit, issued by the Florida Museum of Natural History for $5 a year. It's inexpensive but not instant — processing can take a couple of weeks, so it's worth applying before your trip rather than the morning of. We're happy to point you to the application when you book.

Rules can change — always check current requirements before you go.
